The Aiyagari model as a Markov chain

A household earns labor income that follows an AR(1) process and cannot insure against it, so it saves a buffer of assets. Discretizing the income process with the Rouwenhorst method turns it into a finite Markov chain, and once you add the household's saving rule, the pair (assets, income) is a Markov chain too. That chain has a single stationary distribution, which is the economy's cross-section of wealth. Prices adjust until the capital households choose to hold equals what firms want to use. Everything below is computed when you press Solve: the value-function iteration, the stationary distribution, and the search for the market-clearing interest rate.

1. Discretizing the income process

The income process zt = ρ zt−1 + εt is approximated by a finite Markov chain using Rouwenhorst's method, which stays accurate even when ρ is close to one (Kopecky and Suen, 2010). The transition matrix P built this way has a useful property: its eigenvalues are ρk for k = 0, …, ne−1. The second-largest is ρ, so the spectral gap is 1 − ρ. That gap sets how quickly the chain forgets its starting state, so in this model persistence and mixing speed are the same number.

2. Convergence to the stationary distribution

Start the population from any cross-section and iterate the (assets, income) chain forward. It converges to the same stationary distribution μ* no matter where it began, which is the ergodic theorem for finite Markov chains (Levin, Peres and Wilmer, 2017). The three curves below start from extreme cases: everyone poor, everyone rich, everyone in the middle. They all reach the same floor. The distance to μ* shrinks geometrically, dt ≈ C·|λ₂|t, and the constant C depends on the starting point, which is why the curves sit at different heights before they meet. The decay rate here (|λ₂| ≈ 0.98) is slower than the income chain's ρ = 0.90: the slow part is wealth accumulation, not income, because it takes many periods to save from a corner of the asset grid out to the stationary spread.

3. The wealth distribution

Because the income shock cannot be insured, households hold a buffer of assets, and some end up against the borrowing limit. The stationary distribution is right-skewed, with a concentration of constrained households at the constraint and a thin upper tail. Larger or more persistent shocks raise both aggregate capital and wealth inequality. The density is drawn as a histogram on evenly spaced wealth bins; the asset grid itself is finer near the constraint, so plotting the raw mass at each grid point would misrepresent the shape.

4. Policy functions

Each line is one income state. The saving rule a′(a, e) says how much wealth a household carries into next period. Where it crosses the 45-degree line is the wealth that income level drifts toward: to the left of the crossing the rule sits above the line and wealth grows, to the right it sits below and wealth is drawn down. For the lowest income states at low wealth the rule lies flat on the borrowing limit, where the household would like to borrow against future income but cannot, so it saves nothing and consumes all it has. The vertical gaps between the curves are the precautionary buffer kept against future low-income spells. Consumption c(a, e) rises with both wealth and income and is concave in wealth: the marginal propensity to consume is steep when poor and flattens when rich, so a household near the constraint spends almost all of an extra dollar while a wealthy one saves most of it. That concavity is the signature of buffer-stock behavior, and it is what a representative-agent model with linear consumption misses.

5. Notes and references

What the interest rate means, and why it can be negative

r is the net real return on capital: its marginal product minus depreciation, MPK − δ. It is the rate at which a household moves consumption from this period to the next by saving. It is a safe return in this model (every saver earns the same r); the risk is in labor income, not in the asset. So it is closer to a real bank or bond rate than to the stock market.

If risk could be insured, the equilibrium rate would be 1/β − 1, where impatience just offsets the wish to smooth consumption. Insurance is missing here, so households save more than that as a precaution. The extra saving raises aggregate capital and lowers its marginal product, so r settles below 1/β − 1. The difference is the precautionary wedge shown in the summary above. When the income risk is large enough, capital becomes abundant enough that its marginal product falls below δ and r turns negative: saving loses value over time, but households still do it because capital is the only store of value available. The rate cannot fall below −δ, where capital demand becomes unbounded. This below-1/β−1 result is the point of Aiyagari (1994) and Huggett (1993).

How the parameters change the equilibrium

parameterincrease it, and…
β  discount factorhouseholds are more patient and save more, so r* falls and K* rises.
γ  risk aversionthe precautionary motive is stronger, so r* falls and K* and inequality rise.
ρ  persistenceshocks last longer and are harder to self-insure, so r* falls and K* rises; the chain also mixes more slowly (the gap 1−ρ shrinks).
σ  shock sizeincome is riskier, so r* falls and K* and inequality rise.
ne  income statesthe Rouwenhorst approximation gets finer; results stop moving once it is large enough.
na  asset gridthe policy, distribution and r* get more accurate, at the cost of a slower solve.
